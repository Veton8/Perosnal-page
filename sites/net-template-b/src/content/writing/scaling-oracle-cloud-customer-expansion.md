When I joined Oracle Cloud Infrastructure in 2020 as VP of Customer Experience, the honest assessment was this: Oracle was a late comer to cloud. AWS and Azure had years of head start, massive ecosystems, and the mindshare of every CTO in every boardroom. We had to play catch-up.

Two years later, the business had grown from approximately $200M ARR to over $1.25B ARR. Here's what we did and what I learned.

## Start With the Customer, Not the Product

The instinct at any company playing catch-up is to lead with product features. "Our cloud does X better than their cloud." That's a losing game when you're behind on ecosystem and integrations.

Instead, we led with the customer's existing reality. Most of our top 200 accounts already ran Oracle applications — databases, ERP, financials. The migration conversation wasn't "switch to our cloud." It was "let's move the Oracle applications you already depend on to a cloud that's optimized for them."

That reframe changed everything. We weren't competing head-to-head with AWS on generic compute. We were solving a specific, painful problem that AWS couldn't solve as well: running Oracle workloads in the cloud.

## Build the Team Before You Scale the Revenue

Before we could grow, we needed people. I built out a customer experience organization that included both pre-sales and post-sales delivery capability — solutions engineers, architects, customer success managers, and critical situation support.

The key decision was making pre-sales and post-sales one team. In most cloud companies, these are separate organizations with separate incentives. That creates a hand-off gap where customers fall through. By owning the full lifecycle, we could promise a customer something in the sales cycle and actually deliver it in implementation.

## Workload Migration Is a Trust Exercise

Migrating an enterprise's critical workloads from on-premises to cloud is not a technology problem. It's a trust problem. The CIO is betting their job on the migration going smoothly. The DBA who's managed that on-prem database for fifteen years is terrified of losing control.

We invested heavily in migration support — not just tooling, but people who would sit with the customer's team, run the migration alongside them, and stay through the first 90 days of production. That human investment was expensive. It was also the single biggest driver of expansion revenue. Once a customer trusted us with one workload, they brought us the next five.

## Dedicated Regions Changed the Game

One of the outcomes I'm most proud of is landing the first two Oracle Dedicated Region deployments. These are full Oracle Cloud environments deployed inside a customer's own data center — sovereign cloud before "sovereign cloud" became a buzzword.

This mattered because it solved the hardest objection in enterprise cloud sales: "I can't put my data in your data center." With Dedicated Regions, they didn't have to. The cloud came to them.

## The Lesson

Playing catch-up in cloud isn't about matching the leader feature-for-feature. It's about finding the wedge — the specific problem you solve better than anyone else — and then executing relentlessly on customer trust. The revenue follows the trust, not the other way around.
