I've built and led teams in Australia, Japan, Saudi Arabia, Canada, and the United States. I grew up in Cairo and Rio de Janeiro. I speak five languages. And after thirty years of cross-cultural leadership, here's what I know: most of what people teach about "cultural differences in business" is either obvious or wrong.

The real lessons are subtler. Here's what actually mattered.

## Japan: Precision Is Respect

I've been to [Japan](/travel/japan/) over ten times — Tokyo, Okinawa, Kyoto. At Microsoft, I ran field enablement for the Japan subsidiary, a business generating over $1B. At VMware, I built partner capacity through Hitachi.

The lesson from Japan isn't "the Japanese are formal" — every business book tells you that. The lesson is that precision is a form of respect. When a Japanese counterpart prepares a detailed agenda for a meeting, they're not being rigid. They're showing you that they value your time enough to plan for it. When they pause before answering, they're not hesitating. They're thinking — because giving a careless answer would be disrespectful.

Once I started matching that precision — preparing thoroughly, speaking carefully, following up exactly when I said I would — every door opened.

## Saudi Arabia: Relationships First, Always

I was moved from Charlotte, NC to Riyadh to build and run [Microsoft Consulting in the Middle East and Africa](/travel/saudi-arabia/). My teams spanned Saudi Arabia, UAE, Egypt, and South Africa. Key customers included STC, Aramco, and Citibank.

In Saudi Arabia, the business meeting starts an hour before the business meeting. The tea, the conversation about family, the asking after people you met last time — this isn't small talk. This is the work. The deal gets done because the relationship is real. Skip the relationship and you'll never understand why your perfect proposal got passed over for someone with a worse deck and better trust.

My Arabic helped. My Egyptian background opened doors. But earning trust still required showing up and delivering, every time.

## Canada: The Quiet Intensity

My third international move with Microsoft was from Riyadh to Paris to Toronto. I ran [Microsoft Consulting Services in Canada](/travel/canada/), working with BMO, TD, and Toronto Power, with teams spanning Toronto, Vancouver, Calgary, and Montreal.

Canadians get stereotyped as polite and laid-back. That's half the story. Canadian business culture has a quiet intensity — people expect competence, they expect follow-through, and they absolutely will not give you a second chance to make a first impression. The politeness is the surface. Underneath it is a standard every bit as demanding as Silicon Valley, just with better manners.

## Australia: Hustle and Figure It Out

My career started in [Canberra](/travel/australia/) — at HiSoft, a systems integrator that did Federal Government contracts. I was a solutions architect handling pre-sales and post-sales. I started as a support engineer and QA lead, moved into development, and hacked games on the side.

Australia taught me how to hustle. Canberra in the early 1990s was a small market. You didn't have the luxury of specialization. You did whatever the customer needed, you figured it out as you went, and you never pretended to know something you didn't. That directness — that willingness to say "I don't know yet, but I'll find out" — has served me better than any MBA.

## The Through-Line

Every culture defines trust differently. In Japan, trust is precision and consistency. In Saudi Arabia, trust is relationship and presence. In Canada, trust is competence and follow-through. In Australia, trust is honesty and hustle.

But here's the thing that's the same everywhere: trust is earned by doing what you said you'd do. The words might be different. The meals might be different. The timelines might be different. But the principle is universal.

If you're leading across cultures, stop trying to memorize cultural norms from a book. Instead, ask yourself one question every morning: did I do what I said I'd do? If the answer is yes, the rest works itself out.
